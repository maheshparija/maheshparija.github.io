My research interests are in earthquake hazard, crustal structure, earthquake source mechanisms, stress transfer, seismic attenuation, seismic precursors, and fluid-injection-induced seismicity. Since 2023, I have operated the Hampton Roads Seismic Network in southeastern Virginia, which is used to identify, locate, and study earthquakes across southeastern Virginia, including those linked to the old Paleozoic fault systems of the Atlantic Coastal Plain.

In the past, my research has focused on studying the crustal structure and seismic hazard of the Indian northwest Himalayas, including the crustal velocity structure, wave attenuation and partial melts, stress transfer along the Himalayan thrust fault systems, fault kinematics, and also identifying earthquake precursors in the eastern Himalaya and, more recently, on understanding the complex intraplate earthquake sources in eastern North America. I also apply and develop machine learning and deep learning methods to improve earthquake source characterization in noisy, low signal-to-noise urban environments.

Research Interests

Broadly, I'm interested in how earthquakes happen, how stress builds up in the Earth's crust, how it's released, and what warning signs, if any, come before a large event. My early work focused on the Himalaya, one of the most seismically active mountain belts on Earth, where I studied the lithosphere's structure and composition, how stress is transferred from one fault to another after an earthquake, and patterns in smaller earthquakes that might hint at a bigger one coming. More recently, my focus has shifted to a related but different question: how human activities, like injecting fluid underground, can trigger earthquakes in places that are otherwise seismically quiet.

  1. Crustal structure & tectonics — mapping what the crust beneath active mountain belts like the Himalaya is made of, and how that structure relates to earthquake risk.
  2. Earthquake stress transfer — understanding how one earthquake changes the stress on nearby faults, and what that means for future rupture.
  3. Earthquake precursors & hazard — looking for patterns in seismicity, like swarms or quiet periods, that might signal a fault getting closer to failure.
  4. Induced seismicity — studying how fluid injection and aquifer recharge can trigger earthquakes, and how to monitor and manage that risk.
  5. Machine learning for seismology — using deep learning to catch smaller, quieter earthquakes that traditional methods miss.

Current Projects

Earthquake monitoring in Hampton Roads, Virginia

Understanding the distribution and origin of microseismicity in stable continental interiors is critical for accurate seismic hazard assessment and the safe implementation of energy infrastructure projects in densely populated regions, such as Hampton Roads. I operate the Hampton Roads seismic network, incorporated in 2021, which monitors the region in real time to detect and locate microseismicity in southeastern Virginia and the Virginia Coastal Plain, which is believed to have many unidentified Paleozoic faults underground that could host earthquakes with capabilities similar to the 1886 M 7.3 Charleston, South Carolina earthquake.

I have applied and developed new tools to better characterize the earthquake source in the region, which is extremely important for future seismic hazard assessments and supports safer management of the Hampton Roads groundwater resources.

Mapping active faults in southwestern Virginia

The Giles County Seismic Zone in southwestern Virginia is one of the more seismically active areas in the eastern U.S., but its fault structure has never been well mapped. Recently, I, along with one of my departmental colleagues, operated a 10-station dense STRYDE nodal seismic network to monitor and locate seismic activity across the Giles County Seismic Zone. This experiment was conducted with the objective of testing whether modern-day monitoring tools and techniques can be applied to detect small micro-earthquakes in an intraplate setting with uneven microseismicity distributions. The study produced promising results and detected and located about 70 hypocenters in the region over a period of two months.
